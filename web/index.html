How this works, and what it cannot tell you

The four steps

For each job type, experience level, jurisdiction and year, the model computes: exposure (what share of the work AI can do at all), then the split between work AI automates and work it merely augments; deployment (a logistic curve capped by each jurisdiction's capacity, because exposure is potential and deployment is what actually happens); displacement (deployed automation becomes a headcount change only as fast as institutions allow); and reinstatement (a lagged offset as cheaper output raises demand and new AI-adjacent roles appear).

Why the entry level takes it hardest

Two mechanisms compound. AI is best at the codified, reviewable work junior staff are hired to do. And junior headcount adjusts through hiring — which has no notice period, no severance and no works council — while senior headcount can only be adjusted by dismissal, which is slow and expensive everywhere. That second mechanism, not the first, is what produces a collapsing junior tier beside a stable experienced one.

Industry and age are lenses over that, not extra grid dimensions

Industry moves two things independently: how fast a sector gets there, and how far it is ever allowed to go. Keeping them apart matters — a hospital can be an eager adopter and still be barred from automating a diagnosis sign-off. Sector demand growth is a third, separate lever on job creation.

Age acts only on what happens to a worker after displacement. How much of a role AI takes, and how quickly an employer can act on it, depend on the job and the jurisdiction — not on the birthday of whoever currently holds it. So age changes recovery and hiring, never displacement. It is written as an amplifier rather than a level shift, because age bias in hiring exists in the no-AI counterfactual too, and this model measures only the difference from it.

Calibration

The experience gradient is fitted, not assumed. Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen measured a ~16% relative employment decline for workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations using ADP payroll microdata, with experienced workers in the same occupations holding steady, adjustment running through employment rather than pay, and declines concentrated where AI automates rather than augments. The model's parameters are set so it reproduces that result, and a test asserts the fit still holds.

Sources

  • Task exposure and the automation/augmentation split — ILO Working Paper 140 (2025), a refined global index built from ~30,000 tasks and ~50,000 human assessments.
  • The experience gradient — Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, “Canaries in the Coal Mine?” (Stanford Digital Economy Lab), ADP microdata through April 2026.
  • Deployment capacity — IMF AI Preparedness Index, 174 economies.
  • Adjustment friction — OECD Indicators of Employment Protection (EPRC, regular contracts).
  • EU deployment timing reflects the AI Act's Annex III high-risk obligations moving from August 2026 to December 2027.

What this is not

A structured scenario, not a forecast. The parameters are anchored on published research, but the combination of them is a modelling judgement, and several inputs are estimates rather than measurements.

Only the generative-AI channel. Robotics and physical automation are out of scope, which is the only reason skilled trades and warehouse work look quiet here.

Job creation is the weakest assumption. History says automation eventually creates as much work as it destroys, but “eventually” has run to decades and five years sits well inside that lag. The model assumes a partial offset. Assume a full one and most cells turn positive by 2030; assume a small one and almost nothing recovers.

The age lens is weaker evidence than the experience lens. The experience gradient is fitted to payroll microdata. The age multipliers are estimates — that re-employment after displacement falls with age is a robust, long-standing finding, but the specific numbers here are not calibrated against any AI-era dataset. The two lenses are not equally grounded, and the shape of the age result should be trusted well before its size.

Everything from 2029 on is extrapolation — the shaded region above. The payroll data the model is fitted to runs to April 2026. A diffusion curve can be defended a couple of years past its last observation; it cannot be defended a decade past. The later years are the model's internal logic running forward with nothing to check it against. Treat the ordering of cells as more trustworthy than the magnitudes, and the magnitudes as more trustworthy than any single number — and apply that far more harshly after 2030 than before it.
